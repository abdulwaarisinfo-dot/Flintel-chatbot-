Rationally, I understand the obvious conclusion: she likes me, she is a good person, and if we get along well, there is no reason for me to reject the relationship because of my past.   But that became the problem.   She did not care.   I did.   And the more I thought about it, the more inferior I felt.   Eventually that inferiority turned into something more complicated.   I suddenly realized that no matter how hard I work now, and no matter what I achieved in China’s exam-driven education system through effort, ability, or luck, I cannot change the childhood I already had.   Life is not a linear scale where every missing item can be filled in later.   Money earned at age twenty-eight can improve the life of twenty-eight-year-old me.   But I cannot wire that money back to myself at age ten, fifteen, or twenty.   By conventional standards, my life today may be quite good. But the embarrassment, fear, limitations, and absences of the past do not automatically disappear just because the present has improved.   Before meeting her, I already knew that families differed enormously in resources.   But that was an abstract idea.   Meeting her made me feel, for the first time, as if I were actually opening the books and comparing accounts.   2. Educational resources: some differences I didn’t even know existed   Education was the most obvious example.   At one point in A’s childhood, her English teacher could be a PhD from one of China’s most elite universities, married to a native English speaker.   The educational resources available to me belonged to a completely different world.   In middle school, my homeroom teacher was very status-conscious. I was admittedly mischievous and not particularly interested in studying at the time, so he disliked me.   Once, he slapped me directly across the face.   I did not dare tell my parents because, based on my experience at the time, I was not confident that their first instinct would be to take my side.   Looking back, the greatest damage those three years did to me was probably not my grades.   It was spending years in an environment where I felt suppressed, dismissed, and deprived of basic dignity.   High school was better in the sense that teachers rarely directly bullied students, but the overall educational quality still disappointed me.   My school was by far the best high school in the county. But my particular class was relatively weak within the school, both in terms of students and teacher assignments.   Some teachers were careless. Some had clearly stopped trying. Others simply lacked experience.   My homeroom teacher was competent academically, but his management style was very typical of the environment: control, obedience, and above all, “do not create trouble for me.”   If students from our class had a conflict with another class, he often preferred to suppress his own students because he could not control the other class.   Two students could commit the same minor violation, but if one was caught by a senior administrator and the other was not, they might receive completely different treatment.   His concern was often less about whether something was actually wrong than whether it had attracted the attention of school leadership.   There were even occasions when he came to class after drinking at lunch, smelling strongly of alcohol.   Our English teacher, meanwhile, barely provided anything resembling serious English instruction for almost three years.   Looking back at my education, high school may have been the stage where the resource quality disappointed me most.   Middle school was the stage where I felt the least dignity.   I used to be almost proud of how “cost-effective” my education had been.   From kindergarten through graduate school, my total tuition was under RMB 50,000. All the tutoring I ever received probably cost less than RMB 10,000.   When preparing for the graduate school entrance exam, I later calculated that my additional expenses were probably only a little over RMB 1,000.   I did not even buy the new iPad I badly wanted at the time, despite its Apple Pencil support being genuinely useful for studying.   I used to see this as evidence that I had completed a reasonably good educational path at extremely low cost.   Now I see another side of it.   Low cost also meant almost no additional educational resources.   Some Chengdu women I know had families that spent well over RMB 100,000 just on tutoring.   And the difference is not only how much money is spent. One hour of tutoring in different cities can buy radically different teachers, information, courses, networks, and horizons.   These constraints affected real decisions.   When applying to universities after the gaokao, China’s national college entrance examination, I barely considered any joint Chinese-foreign programs.   My score was high enough for a cooperative program at a reasonably good public university. As far as I remember, studying abroad was not even mandatory. Tuition was about RMB 30,000 per year.   But the moment I saw that number, I decided it was too expensive.   I chose a much cheaper university that was also clearly a level below.   Later, during my third year of university, I had an opportunity to study abroad on exchange. I genuinely wanted to go.   But I had already decided to prepare for graduate school. I calculated the time, money, and opportunity cost, and gave up the idea.   At the end of my second year, I also knew a student who transferred credits and continued undergraduate study at a good North American university. I researched that option seriously as well.   The cost was simply beyond what my family could bear.   Strictly speaking, I am not even sure these experiences should be called “missed opportunities.”   At the time, they were not really options available to me.   3. Family, security, and how much safety net a child receives   My family environment also shaped the way I think.   To be clear, my family was never among the poorest locally.   But as long as I can remember, my father has had a very volatile temper. It was not directed only at me. He could become angry with relatives, strangers, his own mother, and sometimes even people above him at work.   He also rarely reflected on his own behavior. Throughout my childhood, I almost never heard him voluntarily admit that he had been wrong.   In terms of education, emotional support, and guidance, I do not think he fulfilled the role of a father particularly well.   Instead, I absorbed a considerable amount of his negative emotion.   As a child, I assumed all fathers were like this.   Only later did I discover that they were not.   To be fair, he also did many concrete things for me.   When I was young, he spent years taking me to and from school, first on a motorcycle and later by car. He also handed most of his income to my mother to manage, which of course meant a substantial portion of it was ultimately spent on me.   But there is still something I find difficult to reconcile.   He could spend enormous amounts of time reading fantasy novels, yet apparently never had much interest in learning about child development, parenting, or education.   Perhaps in his environment that was perfectly normal.   But because I later developed a habit of approaching problems through “collect information, analyze it, then make a decision,” I find it difficult not to wonder: if raising a child is one of the longest and most consequential projects in a person’s life, why would someone learn almost nothing about how to do it?   My mother is more complicated.   She has always been extremely frugal. We did not have enormous financial resources, but a large proportion of what the family had was spent on me.   When I was little, she could also be harsh, sometimes even cutting. But as her work environment changed, she gradually became more flexible and much more respectful toward me.   Given the environment and constraints she faced at the time, I think she did quite well.   I have always been grateful to her.   But objective limitations were still limitations.   In high school I had to live near school. The dormitory conditions were poor, and for other reasons my family considered renting an apartment nearby.   A normal apartment near the school cost, as I remember it, a little over RMB 20,000 per year.   That would not be a major expense to me today.   At the time, it was something my family had to think about very seriously.   In the end, I lived in a rural-style self-built house owned by relatives near the school because it was cheap. This kind of arrangement was not unusual in the area.   I was already beginning to feel embarrassed about these differences.   Some classmates had parents drive them to school every day. Some had private transport arrangements. Others simply rented modern apartments near campus.   Between the cluster of self-built houses and the school was a small bridge that could easily flood during heavy rain.   One year, during a severe storm, a grade-level administrator—Mr. W—personally arranged vehicles to help students cross safely.   It was not remotely part of his formal responsibilities.   For some reason, I still remember it many years later.   Perhaps in an environment where resources were already limited, an adult choosing to think one extra step ahead for students felt especially precious.   Earlier in my childhood, things were harder.   My parents worked in rural townships for long periods, so I lived with my grandmother.   She could be very harsh. Sometimes she made me kneel, insulted me, or threatened to abandon me if I did not behave.   A small child cannot reliably tell whether that is merely an angry adult saying something they do not mean.   He only hears: the only person I know and depend on in this city may decide to leave me.   We lived in an old neighborhood on the edge of town. The apartment was not actually tiny, but there was almost nothing in it: two beds, a dining table, and a coffee table.   No television. No sofa. Almost no toys. No children nearby to play with.   The only object that gave me some sense of a larger world was my grandmother’s radio, and even that was not mine.   Later, while my parents were still working outside town, I lived with grandparents and other relatives.   Looking back, I think I spent a surprisingly large part of childhood without a real sense of belonging.   It often felt less like living in my own home than living in somebody else’s.   Eventually my parents worked hard, benefited from some favorable policy and career opportunities, and managed to transfer back to the city.   Only then did our family life gradually stabilize.   Scarcity was never only about money.   It was also about information and the ability to imagine a larger world.   I attended the best middle school and high school in the area, but the social sample available to a child there was still extremely narrow.   Some students did not study seriously at all, but their parents or relatives held influential local government or public-sector positions.   They had lots of spending money, the newest iPhones, and seemed to live comfortably without suffering much academically.   I envied them intensely.   What could a teenager like me see?   Teachers seemed reluctant to offend them. They did not work particularly hard, yet their lives looked good.   At one point I genuinely struggled to understand what studying was even for.   The deeper problem was that many teachers and parents around me also lacked concrete knowledge of the outside world.   Their image of the “best possible job” was often simply a stable and respectable position in the local public sector.   What kinds of careers different universities could lead to, what large corporations were really like, what people actually did in higher levels of government or major institutions, how incomes differed, how career ladders worked—many adults around me could not explain these things either.   So from my teenage perspective, education did not appear to have an enormous payoff.   Locally, I rarely saw people achieve dramatic mobility through education.   Years later, the picture looks different.   Some of the students I envied did successfully inherit the advantages of their families.   Others declined quickly when the environment changed.   Education, meanwhile, was ultimately what allowed me to leave that narrow social sample.   4. Entertainment in a resource-poor environment, and learning to choose the path of least resistance   There is one trait in myself that I used to take pride in, but now view more ambivalently.   I am highly goal-oriented. I am good at collecting information, analyzing possible paths, and finding low-cost solutions.   That ability has contributed enormously to the life I have today.   But I suspect it was originally trained by scarcity.   From a young age, I learned to calculate.   What is worth the money?   What is not?   Which option is safest?   Which risk is too high?   What can be sacrificed so that resources can be concentrated on something more important?   Later I discovered that some people do not have to optimize every step of life this way.   They can learn something simply because they enjoy it.   They can visit somewhere because they are curious.   They can tolerate some wasted money, wasted time, and failed experiments.   I learned very early to treat life as a project requiring continuous cost-benefit analysis.   That skill got me here.   It also cost me experiences that might have mattered.   I have also wondered why I loved the internet and video games so much as a child.   One explanation increasingly makes sense to me: for children growing up in places with limited resources, the internet and video games may be the easiest forms of happiness to access—and among the few areas where the gap between you and a child in a big city can feel relatively small.   Family travel is expensive and requires time.   Museums, exhibitions, classes, clubs, performances, and other activities are difficult to access when the place you live simply does not have many of them.   History often follows paths of least resistance. Mountain towns grow along valleys and flat land. Grass grows through cracks in concrete.   Children also look for the easiest available route to pleasure.   If your parents do not have time to spend summer vacation with you, cannot afford to arrange interesting activities, and your hometown offers little to begin with, then going online becomes the obvious answer.   My mother once said something that still makes me sad many years later:   “We didn’t really expect you to learn that much from those tutoring classes. We mainly wanted you to spend less time online.”   I understand her perspective.   Given their resources, they probably thought it was the responsible choice.   But hidden inside that sentence was another reality:   We do not have enough money or time to entertain the child ourselves. The town does not offer many meaningful alternatives. But we believe the internet is bad, so the only available solution is to fill his time with tutoring.   That is why I still refuse to describe the games of my childhood simply as harmful distractions.   QQ Pet, QQ Tang, CrossFire, Dungeon Fighter Online, League of Legends—these games gave me genuine happiness.   Without them, I am not sure what I would have had to look forward to.   Dungeon Fighter Online occupies a particularly large place in my childhood memories.   My family bought its first computer in 2007. Because the budget was limited and because Chinese electronics markets at the time involved enormous information asymmetries, the computer was absurdly weak by today’s standards: a Tsinghua Tongfang desktop with a 1.6GHz single-core CPU and integrated graphics with 32MB of video memory.   I still remember exactly what it could and could not run.   It could handle QQ Pet, QQ Tang, and Dungeon Fighter.   It could not handle CrossFire, Crazy Racing, or several other games my classmates played together.   When classmates and children in my neighborhood were playing games my computer could not run, I was excluded.   Dungeon Fighter became one of the few games I could participate in consistently.   There was even a small act of kindness inside that game that I still remember.   I had an in-game “master,” an older player who helped teach me the game. Once my account was hacked and all my equipment and gold disappeared.   He simply gave me some gold so I could continue playing and rebuild.   He asked for nothing in return.   Today that seems trivial.   To the child I was, it was real kindness.   There are other small things I still find difficult to dismiss, such as clothing.   Clothes were not necessarily cheap in my hometown. My parents were not completely unwilling to spend money on them, but many of the clothes they bought me were unattractive and badly fitted.   My mother especially liked buying clothes one or even two sizes too large so that I could wear them for several years.   I spent a long time wearing clothes that were obviously too big for me, to the point that I came to believe that was simply the size I was supposed to wear.   Later, when I began buying clothes myself, I initially kept buying the same oversized sizes.   It was only after entering university that I gradually learned how clothing was actually supposed to fit me.   It sounds almost funny now.   But I cannot entirely dismiss what it did to my appearance, self-confidence, and social life at the time.   In middle school I had a crush on a girl. I recently heard that she has gotten married.   She really was beautiful, and I was very foolish back then. I did things that embarrass me to remember.   Looking back, my lack of attractiveness at the time was the product of many things at once: personality, social skills, money, clothing, worldview, and confidence.   I also did not seem to have many friends who actively sought me out.   More often, the people who included me were simply charismatic classmates who got along with everyone and were kind enough to include me too.   5. Scarcity does not automatically disappear when you become an adult   That scarcity mindset continued affecting me even after my family’s finances improved.   My parents actually gave me a reasonable allowance in university. They would sometimes give me extra money during holidays.   By the time I graduated, I somehow had tens of thousands of RMB in savings.   Looking back, I increasingly regret how reluctant I was to spend any of it.   I genuinely needed an external monitor, but never bought one.   My computer could no longer run many popular games, but I never seriously considered replacing it with a better machine.   At one point, my long-distance girlfriend had a relatively long period of free time and came to stay with me.   Hotels near my university cost only a little over RMB 100 per night.   I think we even argued once about accommodation and money.   After roughly two nights, she went to stay in a friend’s university dormitory for several days. When the weekend came, she returned, and I finally booked another hotel.   Yes, I saved some money.   But the absurd part is that I already had tens of thousands of RMB sitting in my account.   Today that amount of money has very little marginal value to me.   At the time, I could not bring myself to spend a tiny part of it on a young and uncomplicated relationship.   Thinking about that still makes me sad.   I also feel that I let her down.   There is another memory from 2018.   My father wanted to replace his car, and I badly hoped he would leave the old one to me.   His stated reason for refusing was that the handbrake did not work particularly well on hills, and he was worried that my limited driving experience would make it dangerous.   But once my mother casually said:   “How would you even pay for the insurance and fuel?”   I have often wondered whether that sentence was closer to the real reason.   Perhaps saying directly, “The family cannot afford to maintain a second car for you,” would have been uncomfortable for everyone.   My father eventually sold the car for only a few thousand RMB, even though I remember the normal market value being closer to RMB 20,000.   It had never been in a major accident. If he had left it to me, I would at least have avoided the risk of buying a used car from a stranger.   At that age I desperately wanted a car of my own.   But I was not an experienced driver, and whenever I drove my father’s car with him sitting in the passenger seat, he would quickly start giving nonstop instructions and shouting.   That made me increasingly nervous behind the wheel.   I was even afraid to rent a car because I felt as though one mistake would make the sky collapse.   Eventually I forced myself to rent shared electric cars once or twice.   I still remember the enormous happiness of driving through the city alone, holding the steering wheel myself.   At the same time I was intensely anxious.   The two emotions coexisted in a very strange way.   I also remember December 2019, the day I finished China’s graduate school entrance examination.   The university I had applied to was not at the very top tier, and after finishing the subject exam I already felt fairly confident that I would get in.   I finished the last paper with time remaining and sat there smiling stupidly to myself.   Even the proctor looked at me a few times.   Afterward I went to the cafeteria and, unusually for me, ordered only one meat dish and one vegetable dish. I probably was not hungry at all.   I went back to my dorm, played the attack-speed/critical-strike Garen build that was popular in League of Legends at the time, kept winning games, and felt incredibly happy.   The next day I had all-you-can-eat hot pot with my roommates.   The day after that I went home.   Then began a nine-month period of basically staying at home.   My parents did not pressure me much during that period. They did not criticize me for spending every day at home, probably because they knew I would soon begin graduate school.   I have always appreciated that.   In April 2020, I began dating a woman I had known before.   We had actually met in 2017, during my first year of university. I think we liked each other even then, but she had a boyfriend at the time and I never said anything.   Later she told me that she had liked me too.   We stayed together throughout my three years of graduate school.   Eventually she went to a very good university in Beijing for a PhD, while I failed to enter a central-government institution I had originally hoped to join, partly because of physical-fitness requirements.   We eventually separated, regretfully.   Looking back, many turning points in life invite the same question:   If I had had a little more money, a little more information, or a little more courage, would everything have gone differently?   There is never an answer.   Housing gives me the same feeling.   My parents actually bought an apartment for me in Chengdu relatively early, but it was extremely far from the city center.   For family reasons, they had less money available than they otherwise might have had, so their limited budget pushed them toward a remote location.   Even they knew it was inconvenient, which was one reason they were reluctant to renovate it. They were not sure I would ultimately remain in Chengdu.   The apartment was already completed in 2018.   Sometimes I think that if it had been renovated, perhaps my long-distance girlfriend and I would at least have had a place of our own in Chengdu.   After I started working, I eventually lived there for a year.   The commute was intolerable.   I sold it and bought the apartment I live in now.   Then the market value of the new apartment fell by hundreds of thousands of RMB within a little over a year.   Sometimes I still think: if my family had had a slightly larger budget when I was a freshman, perhaps they could have bought in a better location and many later decisions would have been different.   But ultimately that is just using knowledge from the present to retry decisions made under constraints in the past.   There are many smaller regrets of the same kind.   While preparing for graduate school, I badly wanted a new iPad with Apple Pencil support. Watching classmates use them for notes, storage, and search, I could see that they were genuinely useful.   I still did not buy one.   During all three years of high school, I used a phone that was already painfully slow. Sometimes opening Taobao took tens of seconds.   I kept using it because I knew my family’s financial situation, and I did not even fully feel that I was entitled to want something better.   My parents also took me traveling very few times during my childhood.   One of the trips I remember most clearly was simply to Chengdu.   By the time A was growing up, her parents had already taken her to most of China’s provincial-level regions.   6. What I really envy is “slack”   None of these things, taken individually, qualifies as serious suffering.   Someone could reasonably say:   So your phone was bad. Your clothes were oversized. You did not travel much. You did not get an iPad. Your computer was weak. You did not study abroad.   So what?   And I understand that reaction.   Compared with people who experienced true poverty, lacked basic education, or struggled to meet basic needs, I was fortunate.   My parents did love me, and within their own abilities and understanding, they gave me a great deal.   I eventually completed graduate school, moved to Chengdu, found a stable job, and now own a home and a car.   My life cannot honestly be reduced to a simple victim narrative.   More importantly, as a child I did not even know what I was missing.   A child’s reference group is extremely narrow.   If everyone around you lives more or less the same way, you do not spend your childhood asking:   Why don’t I travel internationally?   Why don’t I have better English teachers?   Why can’t my father provide more emotional support?   Why has no one told me what other schools, careers, and ways of living exist outside this place?   Many things did not even register as absences.   I did not know they were resources a child could have.   The real change happened after I became an adult.   I left my hometown, entered higher levels of education, started working in Chengdu, and met more and more people whose childhoods had been fundamentally different from mine.   For the first time, I had a real comparison group.   And something painful happened:    I began using the knowledge of my twenty-eight-year-old self to reinterpret the lives of my eight-, fifteen-, and twenty-year-old selves.    Things that once felt simply like “the way life is” suddenly acquired completely different explanations.   Experiences that did not feel especially unfair when I was a child now make me sad.   Choices I once considered natural now look constrained by money, geography, family knowledge, and access to information.   Perhaps that is why this feeling has become so intense.   I did not suddenly lose anything.   I suddenly learned what I had never had.   Eventually I realized that what I envy about A is not really the RMB 1,500 headphones.   It is not the expensive laptop, the restaurants, or the plane tickets.   What I envy is something I would call  slack .   In economic terms, it is a family’s greater capacity to absorb risk.   In sociological terms, it is the room for choice created by economic capital, cultural capital, and social capital acting together.   A child with more resources can seriously consider a semester abroad instead of first calculating whether the expense will burden the family.   When she wants to learn something, she can first ask whether she enjoys it rather than immediately calculating the return on investment.   One failed exam does not necessarily narrow her entire future.   Her parents can tell her what schools, industries, professions, and paths exist in the wider world.   Even if she chooses badly once, the consequences are unlikely to endanger the family’s financial security.   Slack does not mean never thinking about reality.   It means reality gives you a larger margin for error.   My childhood built a different ability in me:   path optimization.   I am very good at defining the objective, collecting information, comparing costs, and finding a route with a high probability of success, low expense, and manageable risk.   I approached graduate school this way.   I approached employment this way.   I approached many major life decisions this way.   This ability is genuinely useful.   It helped me escape the limited environment where I grew up and achieve a degree of upward mobility.   I do not want to deny what that upbringing gave me.   But I eventually discovered the cost.   When you become accustomed to treating life as a continuous optimization problem, it becomes difficult to do something simply because you like it.   It becomes difficult to tolerate experiments with no obvious payoff.   It becomes difficult to waste a little time or money without feeling guilty.   That may be the part that hurts most.   I once believed that if I worked hard enough and filled in the boxes one by one—education, career, income, apartment, car—then eventually I would have caught up.   Later I realized that some advantages do not exist only in the amount of money someone has today.   They exist in the number of choices they had over the previous twenty years.   The mistakes they were allowed to make.   The world they were able to see.   And the confidence that they did not have to get every single step right.   Money matters enormously.   But money is only the most visible part.   I still do not entirely know how to live with these feelings.   I know it would be unfair to judge my parents twenty years ago using what I know today.   I cannot reasonably take the childhood of an upper-middle-class Chengdu family and expect a normal family in a relatively underdeveloped part of China to have reproduced it decades ago.   I also know that A has shown me only selected parts of her life, while I am comparing those parts against the complete backend database of my own twenty-eight years.   That comparison is structurally unfair.   I understand all of this rationally.   But rational understanding does not automatically erase emotion.   What troubles me more is that this feeling eventually stopped being only about the past.   It entered the relationship itself.   She repeatedly told me that she did not care about these differences.   She clearly told me that she liked me.   Yet I became increasingly obsessed with the difference between our backgrounds.   I magnified things she was not even using to judge me.   Eventually I began questioning whether I was really suitable for her.   Looking back, what might have damaged the relationship was never her opinion of my past.   It was that I turned those differences into an evaluation of myself.   In a sense, my own class shame began sabotaging a relationship that may not have had any serious problem in the first place.   That is probably more important for me to reflect on than the laptop I never had or the exchange program I never joined.   The resource gap of the past cannot be changed.   But if I continue using past scarcity to define who I am today, then the past is no longer merely past.   It continues participating in the present.   It may even determine what kinds of relationships I allow myself to have in the future.   I could not choose where I was born at eight.   I cannot go back and choose different teachers at fifteen.   But at twenty-eight, I should at least begin learning to distinguish between the losses that genuinely belong to the past and the restrictions I am still recreating in the present.   7. It has also changed the way I think about having children   All of this has also changed how I think about marriage and parenthood.   I once came across a question in the ethics of procreation: before a person exists, parents obviously cannot ask whether that person wants to be born.   That does not prove that having children is wrong.   But it made me realize something simple: before a child exists, there is no child waiting to have a need fulfilled. The desire for a child necessarily begins with the parents.   If I could somehow stand outside my own life before any of it had begun, knowing everything I have experienced so far, and choose whether to start it, I think I might choose not to be born.   I do not mean that I want to die now. Those are two very different questions.   I mean only that if this life had never begun, there would have been no version of me who felt deprived by its absence.   Because of that, I cannot confidently say whether I will eventually have children.   If I do, I hope I can at least be honest that the decision will begin with  my own need .   Perhaps I will fear my own mortality and want a sense of continuation. Perhaps I will want family, companionship, or meaning.   There may be love in that decision, but there will also be something I want for myself.   And precisely because the decision begins with the parent, I increasingly believe that the parent’s obligation to the child is much greater than “I gave you life, so you owe me gratitude.”   If I ever become a father, I hope I can give my child more security, respect, information, choice, and room to fail than I had.   I hope they do not feel that every step of life has to be optimized perfectly.   But even knowing that, I still have not decided whether I want children.   And because I have not answered that question, I am not entirely sure I have answered the question of marriage either.   8. I cannot send money back to my younger self   Today I can buy the computer I could not afford as a child.   I can buy the iPad I refused to buy in university.   I can drive my own car.   I can travel.   I can go to places that once seemed impossibly far away.   But I cannot go back to age ten and give myself a home that feels completely safe.   I cannot return to fifteen and give myself better educational resources and a stronger sense of dignity.   I cannot return to eighteen and fill out university applications without worrying about that RMB 30,000 annual tuition bill.   I cannot return to twenty and tell the version of myself calculating the cost of studying abroad:   “Go.   Don’t worry about the money.”   Maybe what makes me sad is not what I have today.   It is that I finally understand that some people really were allowed to grow up that way.
